# Safety notes on clinical-study articles: what premium skincare brands do, what the rules say, what to write

Date: 2026-09-30. Prepared for Malcolm Smith (Skingenetix). Research agent, read-only; no other files changed.

**How to read the "verified" marks.** "Opened" means I downloaded the page's HTML and read the text myself
(curl, then searched the text), so the quotes are verbatim. "Search only" means a search engine summary said
something and I did not open the page, so treat it as a lead, not evidence. Anything I could not open is
listed as unverified.

---

## 1. Answer in one paragraph

**Yes, add a short safety note, but for a narrower reason than the audit gave.** Nothing I could find, in
Google's rater guidelines or in EU cosmetics law, _requires_ a safety block on an educational article. The
audit check "Safety information present" is a reasonable proxy for "Trust", not a quoted rule. The real
reasons to add it are: (1) brands that write their own ingredient education mostly do it (5 of the 7 brands
whose education articles I could read carry something, in very different amounts; 2 carry nothing I could
find), (2) your pages sit one click from a product page and compare a trial's side effects with no usage
guidance, (3) PDRN is salmon-derived and the peer-reviewed literature does flag fish allergy, and almost no
consumer salmon-PDRN brand says so, which is an easy way to be ahead, and (4) it costs about 80 words. Keep
it calm, factual, and free of any "safe" wording, because in the EU a safety statement is itself a claim you
must be able to back.

### 2b. What the table says

- **Product pages: near-universal.** Every brand whose product page I could read in full had a precautions
  line. The shared pattern is "patch test, avoid eyes, stop if irritation, see a doctor if it persists,
  unbroken skin only". Placement is a labelled block under the ingredient list or a collapsible panel. Medik8
  and The Ordinary also link to a patch-test how-to page.
- **Educational articles: common, not universal, and always short.** Of the 7 brands where I could read an
  education article (Medik8, INKEY, Timeless, The Ordinary, Murad, Dr. Dennis Gross, Drunk Elephant; Sunday
  Riley's article was too thin to judge): Medik8, INKEY, Timeless and Murad (US retinol guide) carry a real
  note; The Ordinary carries one sentence in one article and none in another; Murad's UK article has none; Dr.
  Dennis Gross and Drunk Elephant carry none that I could find. So "it is best practice" is fair for brands
  that write about a product's actives, but I cannot honestly call it universal. The fair claim is "common
  among the brands that publish detailed ingredient education, and the trend is towards more of it, not less".
- **Three placement patterns**, all short: (1) one inline paragraph at the end of the "how to use" section
  (Medik8, The Ordinary); (2) a named section (INKEY: "Side Effects & Precautions", "Who Should Take Extra
  Care"); (3) an FAQ question (Timeless, INKEY). Nobody uses a footer-only legal disclaimer for this. None of
  the brands I read use a pop-up or a banner.
- **No brand I read puts "clinically proven safe" on an education article.** The one place "safe" appears is
  INKEY's "Safe for use during pregnancy and breastfeeding", and Timeless's "suitable for use during
  pregnancy". Those are product claims, and they are the exact kind of statement EU claims law asks brands to
  substantiate (see section 3).
- **Gap:** none of these are "clinical-study appraisal" pages like yours. Those pages are a different genre (closer to a review article), and I found no premium-brand equivalent to compare against. That is a real limit on "what brands do".

### 3b. What that means for your note

1. **The legal duty sits on the label and the product page, not on the article.** The law I found does not say
   a blog article must carry a safety block. Whether a trial-appraisal article counts as "advertising" under
   Art 20 is a legal question I could not settle; the article links to products, so it is safest to assume it
   does. **Action for you:** the six-language product pages (see GPSR point above) are the place where the
   precautions must exist in a language readers understand. The article note should point to them, not replace
   them.
2. **Wording risks**, each traceable to the text above:
   - **"Safe" is a claim.** "Safe for use in pregnancy", "clinically proven safe", "gentle on all skin",
     "hypoallergenic", "dermatologically tested" each imply evidence you must hold (criteria 3.1, 4.1;
     technical document Annex IV). Avoid them unless your safety assessor's file supports the exact phrase.
     Two brands above use pregnancy wording; I am not recommending copying it.
   - **Don't carry a trial's results over to your product.** A study appraisal will often be about a peptide
     at a concentration or in a vehicle different from yours. The note must give _usage guidance for your
     product_, never "the study found it well tolerated, so ours is too" (criteria 2.2 and 3.6).
   - **Don't drift into medicine.** The cosmetic definition is about cleaning, changing appearance,
     protecting, keeping in good condition. "Treats", "heals", "for eczema/rosacea/wounds" would push a page
     toward a medicinal claim. The guidance "if you have a skin condition, ask your doctor" is fine because it
     points away from self-treatment. I did not open the medicines directive, so I cannot say exactly where
     the line sits.
   - **Warnings do not protect the claims around them.** Art 3 says so directly.
3. **The note must match the actual label and safety file.** If your CPSR (cosmetic safety report) or label
   already says something about pregnancy, children, eyes, or sun, use the same wording. I found no primary
   source on GHK-Cu and pregnancy, so I am not suggesting any wording on that. Ask your safety assessor.
4. **UK and US.** I did not cover UK-specific advertising rules (ASA/CAP) or US FDA cosmetic rules. The UK retained text of the Cosmetics Regulation is the same at the points above, but that is not the full UK picture.

**What this does and does not support:**

- **The guidelines contain no line saying "a health page must include safety information."** I searched the
  whole document for it. The audit's criterion is an interpretation of "Trust = accurate, honest, safe,
  reliable", which is a fair reading but should not be described to a client as a Google rule.
- **Skincare serums are probably not "clear YMYL".** The test is whether a careful person would seek an expert to prevent harm. For a cosmetic peptide, mostly not; for a salmon-derived ingredient and a reader with a fish allergy, plausibly yes. So the note helps most on the PDRN pages.
- **A note is not a substitute for authorship and review.** The rater material weights _who wrote it and
  whether they are credible_ (named author, credentials, reviewer) far more than a disclaimer, and the quote
  above shows a generic disclaimer earns nothing. The note helps only because it is specific and useful. Note
  that INKEY, a large brand, credits no medical reviewer on its PDRN article.
- Raters do not score individual pages for real; the guidelines train people who evaluate Google's systems. Adding the note is a trust signal for readers first and a possible help with quality systems second. No evidence it moves rankings.

## 5. PDRN and fish allergy

**Clinical literature (injectables, not creams):**

- A peer-reviewed narrative review: "Individuals with hypersensitivity to fish or fish products should be
  cautious with the use of these DNA drugs sourced from salmon as they may lead to an allergic reaction.
  However, not all patients with fish allergy develop reactions to PN/PDRN. These products are purified by
  filtration during extraction. Prior patch testing with the drug may help identify such patients." And under
  contraindications: "A patch test is warranted in patients who give a history of hypersensitivity to fish."
  The review also lists pregnancy and lactation as contraindications for the injectable. Source:
  Polynucleotides and polydeoxyribonucleotides in dermatology, a narrative review, _Journal of Cutaneous and
  Aesthetic Surgery_:
  https://jcasonline.com/polynucleotides-and-polydeoxyribonucleotides-in-dermatology-a-narrative-review/
  (opened).
- EyeWiki (American Academy of Ophthalmology community resource): "Polynucleotide injections should not be
  offered to patients with fish or shellfish allergies due to their origin. Though this product has been
  purified in the manufacturing process, a small risk of hypersensitivity remains in patients who are allergic
  to fish." https://eyewiki.org/Periorbital_Polynucleotide_Injections (opened).
- **Caveat:** both are about injected products into the dermis. A serum applied to intact skin is a far lower exposure. No source I found tests topical salmon PDRN in fish-allergic people. The caution is therefore precautionary, not proven harm.

## 6. Recommendation and wording

**Recommendation: Yes.** Add one short "Before you try it" block to each study article. Two versions: general
peptide articles, and PDRN articles (general text plus one allergy sentence). This fits what the brand that
explain their actives do, costs little, and closes the audit finding without a legal wall. Do **not**
describe it to yourself or the client as a legal or Google requirement; describe it as good practice on
articles that sit next to a product.

### 6a. Template A: general peptide study article (Argireline, GHK-Cu, Matrixyl, glutathione)

> **Before you try it**
> This article summarises one published study. It is not medical advice, and your own results may differ. Our
> serums are cosmetic products. As with anything new for your skin, try a small amount on your inner forearm
> for 24 hours first, and add one new product at a time. Stop using it if you notice redness, itching, burning
> or swelling. If irritation continues, or if you have a skin condition, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or use
> prescription skin treatments, please ask your doctor or pharmacist before starting. Full directions and
> precautions are on the product page and pack.

About 85 words. Works for all four peptide products because it makes no product-specific claim.

### 6b. Template B: PDRN study article (serum and cream)

> **Before you try it**
> This article summarises one published study. It is not medical advice, and your own results may differ. Our
> PDRN products are cosmetics. **PDRN is derived from salmon. If you have a fish allergy, please speak to your
> doctor before using it, and patch test first.** For everyone: try a small amount on your inner forearm for
> 24 hours first, and add one new product at a time. Stop using it if you notice redness, itching, burning or
> swelling. If irritation continues, or if you have a skin condition, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or use
> prescription skin treatments, please ask your doctor or pharmacist before starting. Full directions and
> precautions are on the product page and pack.

About 105 words.

**Optional extra sentence for the PDRN version, only if your supplier's documents support it:** "The
ingredient is purified, but we cannot rule out a reaction in people who are allergic to fish." Do not write
"purified to remove allergens" or any sentence that promises the PDRN is allergen-free; I found no
documentation of your supplier's process.

### 6c. Placement and length

- **Position:** a visually distinct callout placed after the section that discusses the trial's side effects
  and limits, and before the product / ingredient-hub link. That way the reader meets the trial's own
  side-effect findings, then the practical guidance, then the product link. Not a footer; not a pop-up; not
  above the headline.
- **Length:** 80 to 110 words, five or six sentences, one block. Medik8, INKEY and Timeless keep theirs to a short paragraph or one FAQ answer.
- **Keep the trial's side effects separate.** The note gives _use guidance for your product_; it never restates the trial's tolerability as a safety statement (criteria 2.2 and 3.6).
- **Build it once as a component**, with locale strings, so the six languages cannot drift apart and the wording is edited in one place.
- **Mirror the product page.** The article note points to "the product page and pack"; make sure the product pages in all six locales carry the same precautions (the EU online-offer rule, see section 3).
- **Separate from the note, and stronger for trust:** a named author with credentials and a named reviewer (the rater guidelines weigh authorship and reputation above a disclaimer; see section 4).

### 6d. Translation notes (from general language knowledge, not verified against a source; have a native speaker per locale check them)

- **"Patch test."** The consumer phrase differs by language. In German "Patch-Test" is a clinical term;
  ordinary readers say something closer to "an einer kleinen Hautstelle testen". French, Spanish and Italian
  beauty sites often say "test de tolérance" / "prueba de tolerancia" / "test di tolleranza" as well as "patch
  test". Choose one phrase per locale and use it everywhere.
- **"Fish allergy" is not "seafood allergy" or "shellfish allergy".** Salmon is a fish. Translate literally
  ("Fischallergie", "allergie au poisson", "vissenallergie", "alergia al pescado", "allergia al pesce") and do
  not widen it to seafood. The Pier Augé line says "fish or shellfish"; your note is narrower on purpose.
- **"Speak to your doctor."** Some locales lean on the pharmacist (DE, FR, NL) as the first stop; "doctor or pharmacist" reads naturally there.
- **"Stop using it."** Use the everyday verb ("Anwendung abbrechen", "arrêter l'utilisation", "stop het gebruik", "suspender el uso", "interrompere l'uso"), not a formal legal term.
- **"Cosmetic products."** Keep it plain. Avoid any translation that reads as "treatment" (for example "Behandlung", "traitement", "tratamiento", "trattamento" for the product), because that nudges toward a medicinal claim.
- **"Your own results may differ"** should stay hedged in every language; do not let a translator turn it into a promise.
- **Avoid in every language:** "safe", "proven safe", "hypoallergenic", "dermatologically tested", "suitable for pregnancy". If your label says "suitable for pregnancy", take the exact wording from the label.
- **The GPSR point** (secondary sources) says warning text must be in a language consumers easily understand, as decided by each member state. Your six locales are in scope for product pages.

## 7. Honest uncertainties

1. **"Best practice for premium brands" is only partly true for articles.** Four of the seven brands whose
   education articles I could read carry a real note, a fifth carries one sentence, and two carry none.
   Product pages are near-universal; articles are not. I could not read SkinCeuticals, La Roche-Posay, Paula's
   Choice (site blocks or needs scripts). Augustinus Bader's page was nearly empty. These gaps could change
   the ratio either way.
2. **No like-for-like comparison.** I found no premium brand that publishes trial-by-trial appraisal articles, so the comparison is with ingredient explainers.
3. **The audit's YMYL claim is an interpretation.** The rater guidelines have no "safety information present" criterion. Skincare is at best borderline YMYL.
4. **EU regulation text.** EUR-Lex would not load for me. I read the UK-hosted copy of the retained EU text
   for 1223/2009 and 655/2013 (same wording to my knowledge; not cross-checked against EUR-Lex), and I read
   the GPSR through two cosmetics-law commentaries, not the regulation. Have your responsible person or safety
   assessor confirm the GPSR Art 19 point.
5. **Is a study-appraisal article "advertising"?** I could not resolve this. I recommend treating it as if it is.
6. **The medicinal-product boundary.** I did not open the medicines directive or any national borderline guidance, so "no medical claims" above rests on the cosmetic definition only.
7. **PDRN topical allergy risk is unquantified.** The literature is about injections. I found no study of topical salmon PDRN in fish-allergic people, and the "purification removes proteins" line comes from brands and clinics, not a measured result for your supplier.
8. **Brand pages change.** These were read on 2026-09-30. Brand pages can carry content that loads only with scripts or from separate tabs (medicube, Dr. Althea, Drunk Elephant); "none found" means none in the page text I retrieved.
9. **Pregnancy, copper peptides and other ingredient-specific wording.** I found no primary source, so I wrote none. Your safety assessor owns that.
10. **The translation notes are my own language knowledge**, not sourced. They need native review.
11. **Quote fidelity.** Every quote in the tables was read from the page text I downloaded, except the GPSR Art 19 line, which came from a commentary's quotation of the regulation and is marked as such.
12. **Recency.** The rater guidelines are the 11 September 2025 edition found at Google's current link; a newer edition may exist. The brand pages are dated by the day I read them; none is older than six months except possibly the Timeless and Murad articles, whose dates I did not check.
